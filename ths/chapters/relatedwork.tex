\chapter{Related Work}
\label{ch:relatedwork}

Most rollup research falls into one of two categories: formal security proofs and throughput benchmarks on mainnet. Neither gives you a direct measurement of what happens when things go wrong. This chapter reviews what has been established, notes where it stops short, and describes how this framework fills the gap.

\section{Academic Research on Layer~2 Protocols}

\subsection{Systematization of Knowledge}

Thibault et al.\ provide the most thorough systematisation of Layer~2 blockchain protocols~\cite{sok-layer2}. Their taxonomy organises the design space into four families. Payment channels, exemplified by the Lightning Network and Raiden, enable bidirectional off-chain transfers between fixed pairs of participants. Sidechains, including Plasma and various commit-chain designs, operate as semi-independent chains that periodically anchor their state to the parent chain. Rollups, encompassing both ZK and Optimistic variants, post transaction data on-chain while moving execution off-chain. Hybrid approaches such as Validium and Volition blend on-chain and off-chain data availability to offer a configurable trade-off between cost and security.

The systematisation provides a useful taxonomy and analyses security properties at a theoretical level. What it does not include is any empirical measurement of how these systems behave under failure conditions. The absence of controlled experiments leaves open the practical question of whether theoretical security guarantees translate into usable resilience.

\subsection{ZK Rollup Research}

Bunz et al.\ introduced Bulletproofs, a proof system that generates efficient zero-knowledge range proofs without a trusted setup ceremony~\cite{zk-efficient-proofs}. By eliminating that ceremony, Bulletproofs removed a significant barrier to deploying zero-knowledge schemes in decentralised settings. Subsequent research extended this work toward SNARKs capable of verifying general computation, which is what makes ZK~Rollups over arbitrary smart contract logic possible.

Gluchowski et al.\ published the architectural design of zkSync, a ZK~Rollup built on a PLONK-based proof system~\cite{zksync-paper}. Their contribution centres on proof generation optimisations and strategies for EVM compatibility. The Scroll project builds on this lineage with a Groth16-based verifier~\cite{scroll2023}. The \texttt{ZKVerifier} contract in this thesis draws on patterns from both architectures.

Neither body of work provides a systematic measurement of ZK~Rollup finality behaviour under adverse conditions such as Layer~1 reorganisations or sequencer outages. The performance of ZK~Rollups when something goes wrong remains largely uncharted.

\subsection{Optimistic Rollup Research}

Kalodner et al.\ introduced Arbitrum, one of the earliest Optimistic~Rollups~\cite{arbitrum-original}. Their principal contribution is the interactive fault game: a multi-round dispute protocol that progressively narrows the scope of disagreement until the Layer~1 contract only needs to re-execute a single instruction to determine whether the challenged transition was valid. This reduces the on-chain cost of dispute resolution compared to re-executing the whole batch.

The \texttt{OptimisticVerifier} contract in this thesis incorporates patterns from Optimism Bedrock~\cite{optimism2023bedrock} and Arbitrum Nitro~\cite{nitro2023}, including the configurable challenge period through which commitments must pass before finality. The implementation uses ten blocks---a compressed value that preserves the architectural semantics while keeping experimental runs tractable.

Kalodner et al.\ discuss sequencer liveness and censorship resistance in their original design, but these properties have not been measured in a controlled comparative setting. The cost of failure recovery remains unquantified.

\section{Industry Implementations}

Several production Layer~2 systems are deployed on Ethereum mainnet. This section reviews the most prominent ones as context for the design choices in the framework. Each represents a different engineering approach to the same rollup problem.

\subsection{Arbitrum}

Arbitrum is a widely deployed Optimistic~Rollup. Its architecture features an interactive fraud proof mechanism implemented through the Cannon virtual machine, combined with a seven-day challenge period that gives validators time to detect and dispute invalid state transitions~\cite{cannon2023}. The system includes sequencer failover capabilities that allow a decentralised validator set to resume transaction ordering if the centralised sequencer goes offline. Arbitrum hosts a large ecosystem of decentralised finance protocols and applications.

The engineering complexity of Arbitrum is concentrated in the interactive dispute protocol: the system must correctly sequence all parties, run the bisection on-chain, and determine the correct outcome at minimal gas cost. This is reflected in the multi-year development timeline for the Nitro upgrade.

\subsection{Optimism}

Optimism takes a modular approach within the Optimistic paradigm. Rather than building a single chain, the project developed the OP Stack---a software framework that allows third parties to deploy rollup instances sharing a common security model and bridging infrastructure~\cite{opstack2023}. This has produced independent chains including Base, Zora, and Mode. Optimism emphasises EVM equivalence, meaning existing Ethereum contracts can be deployed with minimal changes. The \texttt{OptimisticVerifier} contract in this thesis borrows the multi-state finality model from Optimism Bedrock, which distinguishes between pending, soft-final, and hard-final commitments.

\subsection{zkSync and StarkNet}
\label{sec:zk-production}

zkSync and StarkNet represent two leading ZK~Rollups pursuing different proof strategies. zkSync uses a PLONK-based proof system and targets full EVM compatibility so developers can write contracts in Solidity~\cite{zksync-paper}. StarkNet uses the Cairo language and a STARK proof system~\cite{starknet2024,cairo2021}. STARKs require no trusted setup and scale efficiently for very large computations, though they produce larger proofs than SNARK counterparts. StarkNet also offers a Volition mode that lets users choose per-transaction whether their data is stored on-chain or off-chain.

The engineering challenge in ZK~Rollups differs from Optimistic systems. Proof generation demands significant computation, and the verification circuit must fit within Ethereum's gas limits. Both zkSync and StarkNet have invested years in hardware acceleration and circuit optimisation. Production documentation and public benchmarks focus on throughput under ideal conditions; behaviour under sequencer failure or Layer~1 disruption receives comparatively little attention.

\section{Gap Analysis and Thesis Contribution}

Reading across this literature, five areas come up repeatedly where empirical data is missing. The framework addresses four of them directly.

\textbf{Finality time under real conditions.} The theoretical finality properties of both rollup families are described clearly in the literature. Actual finality latencies under varying Layer~1 conditions have not been measured side by side. The framework records the submission block and finality block for every batch, giving exact latency histograms across scenarios. The key result is in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}: ZK finality runs around 8~times faster in the demo environment, and the gap widens by several orders of magnitude at production challenge window lengths.

\textbf{Recovery cost after a sequencer crash.} When a sequencer crashes mid-batch, the system loses the in-flight transactions and must restart. The time and data loss associated with this have not been empirically studied. The adversarial test suite includes crash-and-restart scenarios for both sequencer types, and the results confirm that both preserve Layer~1 state integrity while losing pending transactions---with the Optimistic sequencer exposing more transactions to loss because its larger batch threshold causes them to accumulate longer before being drained.

\textbf{Censorship resistance.} The ability of honest users to force-include transactions when a sequencer is unresponsive is discussed theoretically in the Arbitrum and Optimism documentation but has not been measured in a controlled comparative setting. This framework addresses it qualitatively in Chapter~\ref{ch:security} and identifies it as a direction for future empirical work.

\textbf{Controlled head-to-head comparison.} Existing benchmarks evaluate a single rollup architecture in isolation, making comparison difficult because environmental conditions differ. Running both sequencers against the same Layer~1 node simultaneously, with identical block timing and gas pricing, is the core design choice that makes the results in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} directly comparable.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} Existing performance data for Layer~2 systems comes from mainnet observations or proprietary benchmarks that others cannot replicate. This framework is open-source and deployable with one command. Another researcher can verify every number in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} or run the same scenarios with different parameters.

\section{Where the Existing Literature Leaves Off}

Both academic work and industry implementations have made substantial progress on rollup architecture. What the literature has not done is measure both approaches under the same conditions, with the same instrumentation, including scenarios that break things deliberately. Production systems optimise for the normal case, and academic papers analyse theoretical properties. The space between---empirical comparison under adversarial conditions---is what this framework occupies.

Chapter~\ref{ch:framework} describes the design choices that make a controlled comparison possible. Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} covers the code.
